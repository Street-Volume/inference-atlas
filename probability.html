Reference: Theorems 3.3–3.6, expectation and variance

Four short theorems, used constantly from here through Homework 6 (and, later, through Tchebysheff's theorem). None of these are numbered problems — they're the toolbox the problems below reach for.

From the textbook
Theorem 3.3
\(\class{tb}{E(c)=c}\) for any constant \(c\) — a constant has no randomness to average over.
Theorem 3.4
\(\class{tb}{E[cg(Y)]=cE[g(Y)]}\) — a constant factor pulls straight out of an expectation.
Theorem 3.5 (linearity)
\(\class{tb}{E[g_1(Y)+g_2(Y)+\cdots+g_k(Y)]=E[g_1(Y)]+E[g_2(Y)]+\cdots+E[g_k(Y)]}\) — expectation distributes over sums, no independence required.
Theorem 3.6
\(\class{tb}{V(Y)=\sigma^2=E[(Y-\mu)^2]=E(Y^2)-\mu^2}\) — the "computing formula" for variance, almost always easier than expanding \((Y-\mu)^2\) directly.

See these in action: H5·1 and H5·8 use Theorem 3.6 directly; H5·5 and H5·6 prove the linear-transform results \(E(aY+b)=a\mu+b\) and \(V(aY+b)=a^2\sigma^2\) from exactly these four theorems; H6·4 and H6·14 reuse them on a geometric and a Poisson variable respectively.

Diagnostic test reliability

Quiz 4 · Bayes' theorem
Problem

A diagnostic test detects the disease in 90% of people who have it, and correctly clears 90% of people who don't. Only 3% of the population has the disease. If a random person tests positive, find \(P(D\mid T^+)\), the chance they actually have it.

From the textbook
Law of total probability
\(\class{tb}{P(T^+)=P(T^+\mid D)P(D)+P(T^+\mid D^c)P(D^c)}\)
Bayes' theorem
\(\class{tb}{P(D\mid T^+)=\dfrac{P(T^+\mid D)P(D)}{P(T^+)}}\)

Solution

  1. Name the probabilities. \(P(D)=0.03\Rightarrow P(D^c)=0.97\). Sensitivity \(P(T^+\mid D)=0.90\). Specificity \(P(T^-\mid D^c)=0.90\Rightarrow P(T^+\mid D^c)=0.10\) (the false-positive rate).

  2. Total probability of testing positive, mixing both ways a positive test can happen.

    \[ P(T^+)=(0.90)(0.03)+(0.10)(0.97)=0.0270+0.0970=\class{dv}{0.1240} \]

  3. Bayes' theorem — the true-positive share of all positives.

    \[ P(D\mid T^+)=\frac{0.0270}{0.1240}=\frac{27}{124}\approx\class{dv}{0.2177} \]

    Surprisingly low: because the disease is rare (3%), the 9.7% false-positive rate drawn from the huge healthy population (0.10 × 97%) outweighs the 2.7% true positives (0.90 × 3%) even though the test looks "90% accurate" both ways.

Answer
\(P(D\mid T^+)\)0.2177

Airport travelers

Quiz 4 · law of total probability & Bayes
Problem

60% of travelers fly major airlines (\(M\)), 20% private planes (\(P\)), 20% other commercial (\(C\)). Of each group, 40%, 60%, 80% respectively are on business (\(B\)). Find (a) \(P(B)\), (b) \(P(B\cap P)\), (c) \(P(P\mid B)\), (d) \(P(B\mid C)\).

Solution

  1. (a) Law of total probability over the partition \(\{M,P,C\}\).

    \[ P(B)=(0.40)(0.60)+(0.60)(0.20)+(0.80)(0.20)=0.24+0.12+0.16=\class{dv}{0.5200} \]

  2. (b) Multiplicative law — no total probability needed, just one branch.

    \[ P(B\cap P)=P(B\mid P)P(P)=(0.60)(0.20)=\class{dv}{0.1200} \]

  3. (c) Bayes' theorem, reusing (a) and (b).

    \[ P(P\mid B)=\frac{P(B\cap P)}{P(B)}=\frac{0.1200}{0.5200}=\frac{3}{13}\approx\class{dv}{0.2308} \]

  4. (d) Already given directly in the problem statement — no computation needed.

    \[ P(B\mid C)=\class{dv}{0.8000} \]
Answer
(a)0.5200
(b)0.1200
(c)0.2308
(d)0.8000

Problem

  3. Solve for \(k\), then for \(P(L\mid S)=10k\).

    \[ k=\frac{0.008}{2.35}\approx0.003404,\qquad P(L\mid S)=10k\approx\class{dv}{0.0340} \]

\(P(L\mid S)\)0.0340

Matching animal pictures to words

Quiz 4 · counting, derangements
Problem

A child matches 3 animal pictures to 3 words entirely at random. Find the probability distribution of \(Y\), the number of correct matches (as fractions).

From the textbook
Equally likely permutations
All \(3!=6\) assignments of words to pictures are equally likely; count how many give each value of \(Y\).

Solution

  1. List all 6 permutations of correct positions \((1,2,3)\), and count matches in each: \((1,2,3){:}3\), \((1,3,2){:}1\), \((3,2,1){:}1\), \((2,1,3){:}1\), \((2,3,1){:}0\), \((3,1,2){:}0\).

  2. \(Y=3\): only the identity permutation.

    \[ p(3)=\class{dv}{\tfrac16} \]

  3. \(Y=2\) is impossible. If 2 of the 3 positions are correct, the leftover item has nowhere to go but its own correct spot too — forcing all 3 correct, contradicting exactly 2.

    \[ p(2)=\class{dv}{0} \]

  4. \(Y=1\): three permutations swap exactly one pair and fix the third — wait, exactly one fixed point, the other two swapped.

    \[ p(1)=\frac36=\class{dv}{\tfrac12} \]

  5. \(Y=0\): the two full derangements (3-cycles) with no fixed point at all.

    \[ p(0)=\frac26=\class{dv}{\tfrac13} \]

    Check: \(\tfrac13+\tfrac12+0+\tfrac16=1\ \checkmark\)

Answer
Distribution\(p(0)=\) 1/3, \(p(1)=\) 1/2, \(p(2)=\) 0, \(p(3)=\) 1/6

Proving P(∅) = 0 from the axioms

Quiz 4 · axiomatic probability
Problem

Using Axiom 1 (\(P(A)\ge0\)), Axiom 2 (\(P(S)=1\)), and Axiom 3 (countable additivity over disjoint events), prove \(P(\varnothing)=0\).

Proof

  1. Start from Axiom 2.

    \[ P(S)=1 \]

  2. Write \(S\) as a disjoint union with the empty set: \(S=S\cup\varnothing\), and \(S\cap\varnothing=\varnothing\), so they're mutually exclusive.

  3. Apply Axiom 3 to that disjoint union.

    \[ P(S)=P(S)+P(\varnothing) \]

  4. Substitute \(P(S)=1\) from Axiom 2 on both sides.

    \[ 1=1+P(\varnothing) \]

  5. Subtract 1.

    \[ \class{dv}{P(\varnothing)=0} \]
Answer
Result\(P(\varnothing)=0\), proved from Axioms 2 and 3 alone

A coin-matching game

WebAssign 3.3.027
Problem

You and a friend each toss a balanced coin. Both tails: you win \$9. Both heads: you win \$4. Coins don't match: you lose \$2. Find \(E(Y)\) and \(V(Y)\) for your winnings \(Y\). How much should you pay to play if your net winnings are to have mean 0?

From the textbook
Discrete expectation
\(\class{tb}{E(Y)=\sum_y y\,p(y)}\)
Computing variance
\(\class{tb}{V(Y)=E(Y^2)-[E(Y)]^2}\), Theorem 3.6

Solution

  1. Find the outcome probabilities. Two independent fair coins: both tails \(\tfrac14\), both heads \(\tfrac14\), mismatch \(\tfrac12\).

    \[ p(9)=\tfrac14,\quad p(4)=\tfrac14,\quad p(-2)=\tfrac12 \]

  2. Compute \(E(Y)\).

    \[ E(Y)=9\left(\tfrac14\right)+4\left(\tfrac14\right)-2\left(\tfrac12\right)=2.25+1-1=\class{dv}{2.25} \]

    Positive, as the problem promises.

  3. Compute \(E(Y^2)\), then \(V(Y)\).

    \[ E(Y^2)=81\left(\tfrac14\right)+16\left(\tfrac14\right)+4\left(\tfrac12\right)=20.25+4+2=26.25 \] \[ V(Y)=26.25-(2.25)^2=26.25-5.0625=\class{dv}{21.1875} \]

  4. Fair price. Net winnings \(=Y-C\). Mean 0 means \(E(Y)-C=0\), so \(\class{dv}{C=E(Y)=2.25}\).

Answer
\(E(Y)\)\$2.25
\(V(Y)\)21.1875
Fair price\$2.25

Late-night TV preference

WebAssign 3.3.501.XP
Problem

56% of viewers prefer Leno over Letterman. Three viewers are sampled; \(Y\) = number who prefer Leno. (a) Find the distribution of \(Y\). (c) \(P(\text{exactly one prefers Leno})\). (d) Mean and sd of \(Y\). (e) \(P(Y\) within 2 sd of the mean\()\).

From the textbook
Binomial probability function
\(\class{tb}{p(y)=\binom{n}{y}p^y q^{n-y}}\), \(n=3,\ p=0.56\)
Binomial mean and variance
\(\class{tb}{E(Y)=np,\quad V(Y)=npq}\)

Solution

  1. (a) Binomial distribution, \(n=3,\ p=0.56\).

    \[ p(0)=(0.44)^3=0.085184,\quad p(1)=3(0.56)(0.44)^2=0.325248 \] \[ p(2)=3(0.56)^2(0.44)=0.413952,\quad p(3)=(0.56)^3=0.175616 \]

    Sum: \(0.085184+0.325248+0.413952+0.175616=1\ \checkmark\)

  2. (c) Exactly one prefers Leno — this is just \(p(1)\) above.

    \[ p(1)=\class{dv}{0.325248} \]

  3. (d) Mean and standard deviation.

    \[ \mu=np=3(0.56)=1.68,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{npq}=\sqrt{3(0.56)(0.44)}=\sqrt{0.7392}=\class{dv}{0.8598} \]

  4. (e) Within 2 standard deviations. \(\mu\pm2\sigma=1.68\pm1.7195=(-0.04,\,3.40)\). Every possible value \(y=0,1,2,3\) falls inside this range.

    \[ P(-0.04\lt Y\lt3.40)=\class{dv}{1.0000} \]

    Tchebysheff only guarantees at least 0.75 here; the true probability is the full 1, since the whole support fits inside 2 sd of a distribution this concentrated.

Answer
(a)\(p(0{:}3)=\) 0.085184, 0.325248, 0.413952, 0.175616
(c)0.325248
(d)\(\mu=\) 1.6800, \(\sigma=\) 0.8598
(e)1.0000

A tail-sum identity for \(E(Y)\)

WebAssign 3.3.043
Problem

If \(Y\) is discrete with positive probability only on the positive integers, show \(E(Y)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty P(Y\ge k)\).

From the textbook
Swapping the order of a double sum
When every term is nonnegative, \(\class{tb}{\sum_{k=1}^\infty\sum_{j=k}^\infty a_j=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\sum_{k=1}^j a_j}\) — both sides sum the same set of pairs \((j,k)\) with \(1\le k\le j\), just grouped differently.

Proof

  1. Write the tail probability as a sum. \(\class{src}{}\) \(P(Y\ge k)=\sum_{j=k}^\infty P(Y=j)\), so

    \[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P(Y\ge k)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}P(Y=j) \]

  2. Swap the order of summation. The pairs \((j,k)\) being summed are exactly those with \(1\le k\le j\); grouping by \(j\) instead of \(k\):

    \[ =\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{j}P(Y=j) \]

  3. The inner sum has no \(k\)-dependence — it's just \(j\) copies of \(P(Y=j)\).

    \[ =\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\,P(Y=j) \]

  4. That's the definition of \(E(Y)\).

    \[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}P(Y\ge k)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\,P(Y=j)=\class{dv}{E(Y)} \]

    A useful trick: for a nonnegative-integer random variable, you can compute the mean by summing survival probabilities instead of summing \(y\cdot p(y)\) — exactly how reliability and queueing calculations often work.

Answer
Identity\(E(Y)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty P(Y\ge k)\), proved by swapping summation order

Scaling a random variable: \(W=3Y\)

WebAssign 3.3.045
Problem

\(Y\) has mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\); let \(W=3Y\). (a) Compare \(E(W)\) to \(\mu\) by sign of \(\mu\). (b) Find \(E(W)\) using Theorem 3.4. (c)-(d) Find and justify \(V(W)\).

From the textbook
Theorem 3.4
\(\class{tb}{E[cg(Y)]=cE[g(Y)]}\) for constant \(c\)
Variance as spread
Multiplying every outcome by 3 stretches the distribution by a factor of 3, so spread (and hence variance, a squared measure of spread) should grow.

Solution

  1. (a) Sign comparison. \(E(W)=3\mu\). If \(\mu>0\), \(3\mu>\mu\) (larger); if \(\mu<0\), \(3\mu<\mu\) (smaller); if \(\mu=0\), both are 0 (equal).

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Larger if }\mu>0\text{, smaller if }\mu<0\text{, equal if }\mu=0}\)

  2. (b) Apply Theorem 3.4 with \(c=3,\ g(Y)=Y\).

    \[ E(W)=E(3Y)=3E(Y)=\class{dv}{3\mu} \]

    Agrees with (a): the sign-based comparison above is exactly what \(3\mu\) vs. \(\mu\) says.

  3. (c) Variance prediction. Spread argument Stretching every value by 3 increases spread, so \(\class{dv}{V(W)>\sigma^2}\), since the spread of \(W\) has increased.

  4. (d) Prove it. By definition \(V(W)=E\{[W-E(W)]^2\}\). Substitute \(W=3Y\) and \(E(W)=3\mu\):

    \[ V(W)=E\{[3Y-3\mu]^2\}=E[9(Y-\mu)^2]=9\,E[(Y-\mu)^2]=\class{dv}{9\sigma^2} \]

    \(W=3Y\) has variance nine times that of \(Y\) — the square of the scale factor 3, consistent with variance being a squared quantity.

Answer
(a)Larger if \(\mu>0\), smaller if \(\mu<0\), equal if \(\mu=0\)
(b)\(E(W)=\) \(3\mu\), agrees with (a): yes
(c)Larger, since spread increased
(d)\(V(W)=\) \(9\sigma^2\)

The general linear transform \(aY+b\)

WebAssign 3.3.047
Problem

\(Y\) has mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\); \(a,b\) constants. Using Theorems 3.3–3.6, prove (a) \(E(aY+b)=a\mu+b\) and (b) \(V(aY+b)=a^2\sigma^2\).

From the textbook
Theorem 3.5
\(\class{tb}{E[g_1(Y)+g_2(Y)]=E[g_1(Y)]+E[g_2(Y)]}\)
Theorem 3.3
\(\class{tb}{E(c)=c}\) for constant \(c\)

Proof

  1. (a) Split the sum with Theorem 3.5, treating \(aY\) and \(b\) as separate terms.

    \[ E(aY+b)=E(aY)+E(b) \]

  2. Apply Theorem 3.4 to the first term, Theorem 3.3 to the second.

    \[ =aE(Y)+b=\class{dv}{a\mu+b} \]

  3. (b) Start from the definition and substitute \(E(aY+b)=a\mu+b\) from part (a).

    \[ V(aY+b)=E\{[(aY+b)-(a\mu+b)]^2\}=E\{(aY-a\mu)^2\} \]

  4. Factor out \(a^2\) and apply Theorem 3.4 again.

    \[ =E\{a^2(Y-\mu)^2\}=a^2E\{(Y-\mu)^2\}=\class{dv}{a^2\sigma^2} \]

    Shifting by \(b\) never changes spread (only location); scaling by \(a\) changes spread by the squared factor \(a^2\) — the same pattern as H5·5 with \(a=3,b=0\).

Answer
(a)\(E(aY+b)=\) \(a\mu+b\)
(b)\(V(aY+b)=\) \(a^2\sigma^2\)

Lines I and II have the same defect rate. Four regulators are sampled from each (8 total); 6 of the 8 are defective. Find \(P(\text{exactly 3 of the defectives came from line I})\).

Hypergeometric probability function

Which random variables are binomial?

WebAssign 3.4.506.XP
Problem

45% of high school grads took the SAT; 100 are randomly selected. Classify each as binomial (giving \(n,p\) when possible) or not: (a) number who took the SAT, (b) their scores, (c) number scoring above the 1,026 average, (d) time to complete the SAT, (e) number of female grads in the sample.

From the textbook
Binomial requirements
A fixed number \(n\) of independent trials, each a success/failure with the same known probability \(p\) of success. If the "success probability" isn't known or isn't constant across the sampled individuals, or the variable isn't a count of successes at all, it's not binomial — or not binomial with a stated \(p\).

Solution

  1. (a) Number who took the SAT. Each of the 100 sampled grads independently took it with the known probability 0.45.

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Binomial, }n=100,\ p=0.45}\)

  2. (b) The scores themselves. A score isn't a count of successes at all — it's a numerical measurement.

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Not binomial}}\)

  3. (c) Number scoring above average. This is a count of successes, but the "success probability" (fraction of grads scoring above 1,026) isn't the given 0.45 — that 0.45 is the fraction who took the test, a different event entirely.

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Binomial, }n=100,\ p=\text{proportion scoring above }1{,}026\text{ (unknown)}}\)

  4. (d) Time to complete the SAT. A continuous measurement, not a success count.

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Not binomial}}\)

  5. (e) Number of female grads in the sample. A count of successes again, but "success" here means female, a different, unstated proportion.

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Binomial, }n=100,\ p=\text{proportion identifying as female (unknown)}}\)

Answer
(a)Binomial, \(n=100,\ p=0.45\)
(b)Not binomial
(c)Binomial, \(n=100\), \(p=\) proportion scoring above 1,026

⚡ Quant Trading Desk: Esscher Transforms & Option Pricing Derivatives & Volatility Trading
MGFs are the real-valued foundation of the characteristic functions used to price exotic options and calculate Greeks via Fourier transform methods (Carr–Madan, COS method). A closed-form MGF allows instant computation of risk-neutral moments and exponential tilting (the Esscher transform) to shift between physical \(\mathbb{P}\) and risk-neutral \(\mathbb{Q}\) pricing measures. The geometric convergence condition \(qe^t \lt 1\) is mathematically identical to the martingality condition that prevents arbitrage in jump models.
Wall Street Quant Interview (Jane Street / Jump Trading): "Why do we require the moment generating function \(m(t)\) to be defined in an open neighborhood around \(t = 0\)? What happens to asset price tails if the MGF does not exist for \(t > 0\)?"
Desk Takeaway: If \(m(t)\) exists in an open interval around 0, all moments exist and tail probabilities decay exponentially fast. If the MGF fails to exist for any \(t > 0\) (e.g. Student's \(t\) or Pareto return distributions), the asset exhibits heavy/fat tails, meaning extreme market drawdowns occur exponentially more frequently than Gaussian models predict.
