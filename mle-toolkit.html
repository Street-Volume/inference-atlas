The method of maximum likelihood

Wackerly, Mendenhall & Scheaffer §9.7. Suppose the likelihood depends on parameters \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k\). Choose as estimates whichever values maximize \(L(y_1,\dots,y_n\mid\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)\).

The ideaImagine trying every possible value of θ one at a time and asking: "if this were the true value, how likely would it be to see exactly the data I saw?" The MLE is the value that wins that contest — the parameter that makes the observed data look least surprising. With numbers plugged in you get the estimate; written with capital \(Y_i\) it's a random variable, the estimator, which is what you study for bias and variance.

Why maximize ln L instead of L

The natural log is strictly increasing — if \(0\lt a\lt b\) then \(\ln a\lt\ln b\) — so whichever θ makes \(L\) largest also makes \(\ln L\) largest. Taking logs doesn't move the peak, it only reshapes the hill. We do it because \(L\) is a product of \(n\) factors, painful to differentiate, while \(\ln L\) is a sum, differentiated term by term.

Log rules and derivatives used to build and differentiate ln L
Products and powers
\(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\); \(\ln(\prod a_i)=\sum\ln a_i\); \(\ln(a^k)=k\ln a\); \(\ln(1/a)=-\ln a\), so \(\ln(\theta^{-n})=-n\ln\theta\); \(\ln(e^x)=x\); \(\ln1=0\).
Derivatives
\(\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta=\frac1\theta\); \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\theta^k=k\theta^{k-1}\), so \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\theta^{-1}=-\theta^{-2}\); any term without θ (including data terms) has derivative 0.
Finding an MLE in seven steps

1. Likelihood: \(L(\theta)=\prod f(y_i\mid\theta)\); simplify into a few factors. 2. Log-likelihood: \(\ell(\theta)=\ln L(\theta)\); expand with the log rules. 3. Differentiate term by term. 4. Solve \(\ell'(\theta)=0\) for θ. 5. Second derivative: show \(\ell''(\theta)\lt0\) at that point. 6. Global check: confirm it's the only critical point, or that \(\ell'\) is + to the left and − to the right. 7. Write the estimator with capital \(Y_i\).

Two parameters: partial derivatives

With two unknowns, say μ and σ, the log-likelihood is a surface over the (μ, σ) plane, like a mountain over a map. At the summit it's flat in every direction — flat in the μ-direction (\(\partial\ell/\partial\mu=0\)) and flat in the σ-direction (\(\partial\ell/\partial\sigma=0\)). Solving both equations together finds the summit. A partial derivative means: differentiate with respect to one variable while treating the other (and all the data) as fixed numbers.

Second-derivative test, two variables
At a point where both first partials are 0
Compute \(A=\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta_1^2\), \(B=\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta_1\partial\theta_2\), \(C=\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta_2^2\). If \(A\lt0\) and \(AC-B^2\gt0\), it's a local maximum. If \(AC-B^2\lt0\) it's a saddle; if \(A\gt0\) and \(AC-B^2\gt0\), a minimum.
Why
\(A\lt0\) says the surface bends down in the \(\theta_1\) direction. \(AC-B^2\gt0\) together with \(A\lt0\) says it bends down in every direction, not just along the two axes — a summit, not a mountain pass that goes down one way and up another.
InvarianceIf \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of θ and \(t(\theta)\) is a function of θ, the MLE of \(t(\theta)\) is \(t(\hat\theta)\). Changing how you label the parameter doesn't change which model fits the data best — if \(\sigma^2=4\) is the best-fitting variance, \(\sigma=2\) is the best-fitting standard deviation, since they describe the same normal. See 9.96 for this in action.

MLEs depend on the data only through a sufficient statistic

If \(U\) is sufficient, factorization gives \(L(\theta)=g(u,\theta)\,h(y_1,\dots,y_n)\). Maximizing over θ, \(h\) is a fixed positive number with no θ in it, so it never changes where the maximum sits. The maximizing θ is whatever maximizes \(g(u,\theta)\), which depends on the data only through \(u\) — this is why the MLE in 9.82(b) turns out to be a function of the sufficient statistic from 9.82(a). Contrast with the method of moments, whose estimator is not generally a function of the sufficient statistic.

MLEs are not always unbiasedMaximum likelihood looks for the best-fitting parameter, not an estimator that's right on average. Uniform(0, θ): \(\hat\theta=Y_{(n)}\) has \(E(Y_{(n)})=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta\lt\theta\) — the largest observation can never exceed θ, so on average it falls short. Normal: the MLE of \(\sigma^2\) divides by \(n\), so \(E(\hat\sigma^2)=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\lt\sigma^2\) — see Live figure B5. In 9.82 the MLE happens to be unbiased, which is why part (c) answers "yes."
The Weibull family and the power transformation
W

W = Yʳ turns any Weibull into a plain exponential

The density in 9.82 is \(f(y\mid\theta)=\frac1\theta r y^{r-1}e^{-y^r/\theta}\), \(y\gt0\), with \(r\gt0\) known. Set \(r=1\) and it's the exponential with mean θ; set \(r=2\) and it's the Rayleigh density. One family, one knob \(r\), still only one unknown θ. Everything that works for the Rayleigh (6.34, 9.40, 9.58) works again with 2 replaced by \(r\).

W = Yʳ is exponential with mean θ

\(h(y)=y^r\) has \(h'(y)=ry^{r-1}\gt0\) for \(y\gt0\), so \(h\) is increasing and the transformation method (§6.4) applies. Invert \(w=y^r\) to get \(y=w^{1/r}\), so \(\left|\frac{dy}{dw}\right|=\frac1r w^{1/r-1}\). Substituting and multiplying by the Jacobian:

\[ f_W(w)=\frac1\theta\cdot r\cdot w^{\frac{r-1}r}e^{-w/\theta}\cdot\frac1r w^{\frac1r-1} \]

The constants cancel (\(r\cdot\frac1r=1\)) and the powers of \(w\) add to zero: \(\frac{r-1}r+\frac1r-1=\frac{r}{r}-1=0\). So \(w^0=1\) and

Key fact for 9.82(c)

\[ \class{dv}{f_W(w)=\frac1\theta e^{-w/\theta},\quad w\gt0} \] \(W=Y^r\) is exponential with mean θ — whatever \(r\) is, \(Y^r\) always behaves like a plain exponential with mean θ. Averaging the \(Y_i^r\) is like averaging exponentials.

E(Yʳ) and V(Yʳ) by hand, without the gamma function

Integration by parts on \(W\)'s exponential density: \(\int_0^\infty w\,e^{-w/\theta}dw=\theta^2\), so \(E(W)=\theta\). And \(\int_0^\infty w^2e^{-w/\theta}dw=2\theta^3\), so \(E(W^2)=2\theta^2\) and \(V(W)=2\theta^2-\theta^2=\theta^2\).

Moments

\(\class{dv}{E(Y^r)=\theta}\) and \(\class{dv}{V(Y^r)=\theta^2}\). Gamma-kernel shortcut if you remember it: \(\int_0^\infty w^{\alpha-1}e^{-w/\theta}dw=\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^\alpha\), giving the same two numbers in one line each.
