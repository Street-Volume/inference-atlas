Finding an MVUE: sufficiency, completeness and Rao–Blackwell

Wackerly, Mendenhall & Scheaffer (2008), Chapter 9, especially §§9.4–9.5. An MVUE claim is really two separate claims bundled together: that an estimator is unbiased, \(E[T]=\tau(\theta)\), and that it has minimum variance among every unbiased estimator. The second half is the one students skip, and it's the one an exam grader checks for.

The four-step recipe
Likelihood → sufficient statistic U → check completeness → unbiased h(U) → unique MVUE
1. Use the factorization criterion to find a sufficient statistic \(U\) (Theorem 9.4, p. 461). 2. Establish completeness when needed — minimal sufficiency alone is not enough. 3. Construct \(h\) so that \(E[h(U)]=\tau(\theta)\). 4. Combine complete sufficiency with Rao–Blackwell to conclude \(h(U)\) is the unique MVUE.
ChainSufficient ≠ Minimal sufficient ≠ Complete sufficient. Each arrow in the recipe above is a real logical step — none of them is automatic from the last.
Factorization criterion and sufficiency
Theorem 9.4 (p. 461)
\(U\) is sufficient for \(\theta\) when the likelihood factors as \(\class{tb}{L(y_1,\dots,y_n\mid\theta)=g(U,\theta)\,h(y_1,\dots,y_n)}\), where \(h\) does not depend on \(\theta\). The purpose is information reduction: \(U\) summarizes everything in the sample that is relevant to \(\theta\). Example 9.5 (pp. 461–462) illustrates this in an exponential model.
What Rao–Blackwell actually guarantees
Theorem 9.5, Rao–Blackwell (pp. 464–465)
If \(T\) is unbiased for \(\tau(\theta)\) and \(U\) is sufficient for \(\theta\), then \(T^*=E(T\mid U)\) satisfies \(E[T^*]=\tau(\theta)\) and \(Var(T^*)\le Var(T)\). Conditioning on a sufficient statistic preserves unbiasedness and can only reduce (or leave unchanged) the variance.
CautionRao–Blackwell guarantees variance reduction relative to the estimator you started from — not, by itself, a global minimum over every unbiased estimator. That last mile needs completeness.

The role and limitation of minimal sufficiency

A minimal sufficient statistic is a maximally compressed representation of the sample information relevant to \(\theta\). Exercise 9.66 (pp. 471–472) gives a likelihood-ratio method for verifying it. But minimal sufficient ⇏ complete — so \(U\) being minimal sufficient and \(h(U)\) being unbiased do not, by themselves, prove \(h(U)\) is an MVUE.

Completeness: the missing ingredient
Exercise 9.68 (p. 472)
\(U\) is complete when \(E[g(U)]=0\) for every \(\theta\) implies \(g(U)=0\) almost surely. Intuitively: no nontrivial function of \(U\) has expectation zero for every parameter value. This produces uniqueness — if \(g_1(U)\) and \(g_2(U)\) are both unbiased for \(\tau(\theta)\), then \(E[g_1(U)-g_2(U)]=0\) for every \(\theta\), and completeness forces \(g_1(U)=g_2(U)\) almost surely.

The Lehmann–Scheffé logic

  1. Suppose \(U\) is complete and sufficient, and \(h(U)\) is unbiased for \(\tau(\theta)\). Take any other unbiased estimator \(T\).

  2. Rao–Blackwell gives \(T^*=E(T\mid U)\), a function of \(U\) with \(Var(T^*)\le Var(T)\).

  3. Both \(T^*\) and \(h(U)\) are unbiased functions of \(U\) for the same target, so completeness forces \(T^*=h(U)\) almost surely.

  4. Hence \(Var[h(U)]=Var[T^*]\le Var[T]\) for every unbiased \(T\) — exactly the MVUE property.

Lehmann–Scheffé

Complete sufficiency + unbiasedness ⇒ unique MVUE.

Representative example: Weibull, Example 9.7 (pp. 466–467)

This is the same density that drives 9.82 on this site, worked here from the theory side rather than the exam side.

  1. Let \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) be a random sample from \(f(y\mid\theta)=(2y/\theta)\exp(-y^2/\theta)\), \(y\gt0\). The likelihood is

    \[ L(\theta)=\left(\frac2\theta\right)^n\left(\prod y_i\right)\exp\!\left\{-\frac1\theta\sum y_i^2\right\} \]

    Factorization identifies \(U=\sum Y_i^2\) as sufficient. Example 9.7 describes \(U\) as the minimal sufficient statistic; Exercise 9.66(b) verifies that by the likelihood-ratio method.

  2. Let \(W_i=Y_i^2\). The transformation shows \(W_i\) is exponential with mean \(\theta\), so \(E(Y_i^2)=\theta\) and

    \[ E(U)=n\theta\ \Longrightarrow\ E(U/n)=\theta \]

  3. So \(\hat\theta=U/n=\frac1n\sum Y_i^2\) is an unbiased function of the sufficient statistic. The text concludes this is the MVUE, on the background fact that the statistics used in §9.5 additionally have the completeness property (developed in Exercise 9.68).

    In a fully explicit solution, state the complete-sufficiency justification whenever it's available — don't just cite Rao–Blackwell and stop.

Do not memorize"Exponential family ⇒ complete" as an unconditional rule. Many standard one-parameter regular exponential-family models (Bernoulli, Poisson, exponential, and relatives) have natural statistics that are complete and sufficient under the relevant conditions — but membership in the family alone doesn't establish completeness; the natural-parameter-space conditions matter. Multiparameter models need extra care: Example 9.8 (pp. 467–468) treats a normal model with both \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) unknown and gets \(\sum Y_i\) and \(\sum Y_i^2\) as jointly sufficient — don't argue "normal is exponential family, so automatically complete."
Exam-ready solution template

(1) By the factorization criterion, \(U=\dots\) is sufficient for \(\theta\). (2) \(U\) is complete because [state the applicable theorem/conditions]. (3) Since \(E[h(U)]=\tau(\theta)\), \(h(U)\) is unbiased for \(\tau(\theta)\). (4) Therefore, by the complete-sufficiency / Lehmann–Scheffé argument, \(h(U)\) is the unique MVUE of \(\tau(\theta)\).

Avoid this inference: U minimal sufficient + h(U) unbiased ⇏ h(U) is MVUE, in general.

A pooled estimator when the two variances aren't equal

Motivation

The standard two-sample pooled variance assumes \(\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=\sigma^2\) and weights \(S_1^2\) and \(S_2^2\) by their degrees of freedom. But sometimes the two populations don't share a variance outright — instead their variances are known proportional multiples of a single baseline parameter: \(Var(X)=a\sigma^2\) and \(Var(Y)=b\sigma^2\), with \(a,b\gt0\) known constants and \(\sigma^2\) the unknown target.

Derivation

  1. Transform to standard chi-square variables. Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\sim N(\mu_1,a\sigma^2)\) and \(Y_1,\dots,Y_m\sim N(\mu_2,b\sigma^2)\) be independent samples. For any normal sample of size \(k\) with variance \(\sigma_W^2\), \(\frac{(k-1)S_W^2}{\sigma_W^2}\sim\chi^2(k-1)\) (Theorem 7.2). Applying this to each sample:

    \[ \frac{(n-1)S_1^2}{a\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2(n-1),\qquad \frac{(m-1)S_2^2}{b\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2(m-1) \]

  2. Combine the independent chi-squares. By the additive property (Ex. 6.59), \(U_1+U_2\sim\chi^2(\nu_1+\nu_2)\), so

    \[ \frac{(n-1)S_1^2}{a\sigma^2}+\frac{(m-1)S_2^2}{b\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2(n+m-2) \]

    Factoring out the unknown baseline \(1/\sigma^2\):

    \[ \frac1{\sigma^2}\left[\frac{(n-1)S_1^2}{a}+\frac{(m-1)S_2^2}{b}\right]\sim\chi^2(n+m-2) \]

  3. Take expectations. Since \(E[\chi^2(\nu)]=\nu\) (Theorem 4.9),

    \[ E\left[\frac{(n-1)S_1^2/a+(m-1)S_2^2/b}{\sigma^2}\right]=n+m-2 \]

    Dividing by the total degrees of freedom \(n+m-2\) gives an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

Extended pooled estimator

\[ \class{dv}{S_p^2=\frac{\dfrac1a(n-1)S_1^2+\dfrac1b(m-1)S_2^2}{n+m-2}} \]

Set \(a=b=1\) and this collapses back to the familiar equal-variance pooled estimator.

Textbook: Wackerly, Mendenhall and Scheaffer, Mathematical Statistics with Applications, 7th ed. (Cengage); Hillier and Lieberman, Introduction to Operations Research, 10th ed. (McGraw Hill).
