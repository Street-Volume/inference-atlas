Moment-generating functions
Q1

The geometric MGF

WebAssign 3.9.192
Problem

If \(Y\) has a geometric distribution with probability of success \(p\), show that the moment-generating function of \(Y\) is

\[ m(t)=\frac{pe^t}{1-qe^t},\qquad q=1-p,\quad qe^t\lt 1. \]
From the textbook
Definition of the MGF (Section 3.9)
\(\class{tb}{m(t)=E\!\left(e^{tY}\right)=\sum_y e^{ty}p(y)}\)
Geometric probability function (Section 3.5)
\(\class{tb}{p(y)=q^{y-1}p,\quad y=1,2,3,\dots}\) (number of the trial on which the first success occurs)
Geometric series (calculus)
\(\class{tb}{\sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k=\dfrac{1}{1-r}}\) when \(|r|\lt1\)
Quant financeMGFs are the real-valued cousin of the characteristic functions used to price options via Fourier methods (Carr–Madan, COS): a closed-form MGF lets you compute risk-neutral moments and apply exponential tilting (an Esscher transform) to change probability measures. The convergence boundary \(qe^t\lt1\) here is the same kind of constraint that limits how far you can tilt a return distribution before the expectation diverges — exactly what bounds admissible risk-neutral measures in incomplete markets.

Proof

  1. Substitute the geometric p(y) into the definition. MGF definition

    \[ m(t)=E\!\left(e^{tY}\right)=\sum_{y=1}^{\infty}e^{ty}\,q^{y-1}p \]

  2. Make the exponents match. The \(q\) has exponent \(y-1\), so split \(e^{ty}\) the same way: \(e^{ty}=e^{t}\cdot e^{t(y-1)}=e^t\left(e^t\right)^{y-1}\).

    \[ m(t)=\sum_{y=1}^{\infty}p\,e^t\left(e^t\right)^{y-1}q^{y-1}=\sum_{y=1}^{\infty}pe^t\left(qe^t\right)^{y-1} \]

  3. Pull out the constant \(pe^t\) (it does not depend on \(y\)).

    \[ m(t)=pe^t\sum_{y=1}^{\infty}\left(qe^t\right)^{y-1} \]

  4. Re-index. Let \(k=y-1\). When \(y=1\), \(k=0\); as \(y\to\infty\), \(k\to\infty\).

    \[ m(t)=pe^t\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(qe^t\right)^{k} \]

  5. Sum the geometric series with ratio \(r=qe^t\). It converges only when \(qe^t\lt1\), that is \(t\lt-\ln q\). Geometric series

    \[ m(t)=pe^t\cdot\frac{1}{1-qe^t}=\class{dv}{\frac{pe^t}{1-qe^t}} \]

    The condition \(qe^t\lt1\) is part of the answer, not decoration. Live figure A1 shows the series blowing up when you push \(t\) past \(-\ln q\).

Q2

Identify the distribution from its MGF

WebAssign 3.9.198
Problem

Find the distribution of the random variable with each MGF: (a) \(m(t)=\left[\tfrac15e^t+\tfrac45\right]^6\), (b) \(m(t)=\dfrac{e^t}{2-e^t}\), (c) \(m(t)=e^{5(e^t-1)}\).

From the textbook
Uniqueness (Section 3.9)
If two random variables have the same MGF, they have the same distribution. So matching the form identifies the distribution.
MGFs from the table of discrete distributions
Binomial \(\class{tb}{(pe^t+q)^n}\), geometric \(\class{tb}{\dfrac{pe^t}{1-qe^t}}\), Poisson \(\class{tb}{e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}}\)
Quant financeMatching a given MGF to a known family is the same move used to identify a return-generating process from its characteristic function — e.g. confirming log-returns look Variance-Gamma rather than Merton jump-diffusion by matching functional forms. The Poisson MGF \(e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}\) is literally the jump-arrival process in Merton's jump-diffusion option-pricing model; the binomial form underlies the Cox–Ross–Rubinstein binomial tree.

Matching

  1. (a) Binomial form. Line up term by term with \((pe^t+q)^n\):

    \[ \left[\tfrac15e^t+\tfrac45\right]^6\ \leftrightarrow\ \left[pe^t+q\right]^n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{dv}{n=6,\ p=\tfrac15},\ q=\tfrac45 \]

    Check: \(p+q=\tfrac15+\tfrac45=1\). That has to hold or it isn't binomial.

  2. (b) Get the geometric form. The geometric denominator starts with 1, so divide top and bottom by 2:

    \[ \frac{e^t}{2-e^t}=\frac{e^t/2}{(2-e^t)/2}=\frac{\tfrac12e^t}{1-\tfrac12e^t}\ \leftrightarrow\ \frac{pe^t}{1-qe^t}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{p=\tfrac12},\ q=\tfrac12 \]

  3. (c) Poisson form. Match the exponent directly:

    \[ e^{5(e^t-1)}\ \leftrightarrow\ e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{\lambda=5} \]
WebAssign entry
(a)Binomial, \(n=\) 6, \(p=\) 0.2
(b)Geometric, \(p=\) 0.5
(c)Poisson, \(\lambda=\) 5
Q3

Moments and distribution from a finite MGF

WebAssign 3.9.200
Problem

Let \(m(t)=\tfrac19e^t+\tfrac69e^{2t}+\tfrac29e^{3t}\). Find (a) \(E(Y)\), (b) \(V(Y)\), (c) the distribution of \(Y\).

From the textbook
Moments from the MGF (Section 3.9)
\(\class{tb}{\mu_k'=E\!\left(Y^k\right)=m^{(k)}(0)}\), the \(k\)th derivative evaluated at \(t=0\).
Variance shortcut (Section 3.3)
\(\class{tb}{V(Y)=E\!\left(Y^2\right)-\left[E(Y)\right]^2}\)
Chain rule
\(\class{tb}{\dfrac{d}{dt}e^{at}=ae^{at}}\)
Quant financeTaking derivatives of an MGF at \(t=0\) to recover mean and variance is exactly how risk-neutral moments (expected return, variance, skew) are extracted from option prices in model-free implied-moment indices like the CBOE SKEW. Portfolio risk engines run the same trick on a portfolio's characteristic function to get moments for a Cornish–Fisher VaR adjustment without ever simulating the full return distribution.

Solution

  1. First derivative, term by term with \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{at}=ae^{at}\):

    \[ m'(t)=\tfrac19(1)e^{t}+\tfrac69(2)e^{2t}+\tfrac29(3)e^{3t}=\tfrac19e^t+\tfrac{12}{9}e^{2t}+\tfrac69e^{3t} \]

  2. (a) Evaluate at 0. Every \(e^{a\cdot0}=1\). Moments from MGF

    \[ E(Y)=m'(0)=\frac{1+12+6}{9}=\class{dv}{\frac{19}{9}}\approx2.1111 \]

  3. Second derivative, differentiate \(m'(t)\) again:

    \[ m''(t)=\tfrac19(1)e^t+\tfrac{12}{9}(2)e^{2t}+\tfrac69(3)e^{3t}=\tfrac19e^t+\tfrac{24}{9}e^{2t}+\tfrac{18}{9}e^{3t} \] \[ E\!\left(Y^2\right)=m''(0)=\frac{1+24+18}{9}=\frac{43}{9} \]

  4. (b) Variance. Put both fractions over 81: \(\tfrac{43}{9}=\tfrac{387}{81}\) and \(\left(\tfrac{19}{9}\right)^2=\tfrac{361}{81}\). Variance shortcut

    \[ V(Y)=\frac{387}{81}-\frac{361}{81}=\class{dv}{\frac{26}{81}}\approx0.3210 \]

  5. (c) Read off the distribution. For a discrete \(Y\), \(m(t)=\sum_y p(y)e^{ty}\). The coefficient of \(e^{yt}\) is \(p(y)\):

    \[ \class{dv}{p(1)=\tfrac19,\quad p(2)=\tfrac69=\tfrac23,\quad p(3)=\tfrac29} \]

    Check: \(\tfrac19+\tfrac69+\tfrac29=1\), and \(\sum y\,p(y)=\tfrac19+\tfrac{12}9+\tfrac69=\tfrac{19}9\), which matches (a).

(b) V(Y)26/81
(c)p(1) = 1/9, p(2) = 2/3, p(3) = 2/9

Bounds from mean and variance alone

WebAssign 3.10.212
Problem

\(Y\) has mean \(\mu=15\) and variance \(\sigma^2=4\). Use Tchebysheff's theorem to find (a) a lower bound for \(P(11\lt Y\lt19)\), (b) the value of \(C\) such that \(P(|Y-15|\ge C)\le0.04\).

From the textbook
Tchebysheff's theorem
For any \(k\gt0\): \(\class{tb}{P(|Y-\mu|\lt k\sigma)\ge1-\dfrac1{k^2}}\), equivalently \(\class{tb}{P(|Y-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le\dfrac1{k^2}}\).
Quant financeChebyshev's inequality gives a distribution-free bound on tail probability — valuable in finance precisely because real return distributions are fat-tailed, so normal-based bounds (as in Black–Scholes-style models) understate true risk. Risk managers use Chebyshev-type bounds as a conservative, model-agnostic backstop on VaR when they don't trust — or can't verify — a parametric return distribution.

Solution

  1. Get σ. \(\sigma=\sqrt{4}=2\).

  2. (a) Rewrite the interval as a distance from μ. Subtract 15 throughout:

    \[ 11\lt Y\lt19\iff -4\lt Y-15\lt4\iff|Y-15|\lt4 \]

  3. Find k. Set \(k\sigma=4\): \(k(2)=4\Rightarrow k=2\).

  4. Apply the theorem. Tchebysheff

    \[ P(11\lt Y\lt19)\ge1-\frac1{2^2}=1-\frac14=\class{dv}{0.75} \]

  5. (b) Set the tail bound equal to 0.04 and solve for k.

    \[ \frac1{k^2}=0.04\ \Longrightarrow\ k^2=\frac1{0.04}=25\ \Longrightarrow\ k=5 \]

  6. Convert k to C. \(C=k\sigma=5(2)=\class{dv}{10}\). So \(P(|Y-15|\ge10)\le\tfrac1{25}=0.04\).

(a)0.75
(b) C10
Q5

Three coin tosses against the empirical rule

WebAssign 3.10.218
Problem

A balanced coin is tossed three times. \(Y\) is the number of heads. (a) Find the probability distribution of \(Y\). (b) Find \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). (c) Find the fraction of the distribution within one and within two standard deviations of \(\mu\), and compare with the empirical rule and Tchebysheff's theorem.

From the textbook
Binomial distribution (Section 3.4)
\(\class{tb}{p(y)=\binom ny p^y q^{n-y},\quad \mu=np,\quad \sigma^2=npq}\)
Empirical rule
For mound-shaped distributions: about 68% within \(\mu\pm\sigma\), about 95% within \(\mu\pm2\sigma\).
Tchebysheff
At least \(1-1/k^2\): at least 0 within \(1\sigma\), at least 0.75 within \(2\sigma\).
Quant financeComparing the loose Chebyshev bound to the tighter empirical rule mirrors a real risk-management choice: size capital reserves off a Gaussian assumption (tighter, but wrong in the tails) or off a distribution-free Chebyshev bound (looser, but safe under fat tails)? Backtesting a VaR model is essentially asking which of these two guarantees actually held over realized P&L.

Solution

  1. (a) Binomial with n = 3, p = 0.5. Every outcome has \(p^yq^{3-y}=(0.5)^3=\tfrac18\), so only the binomial coefficient changes. Binomial p(y)

    \[ p(0)=\tbinom30\tfrac18=\tfrac18,\quad p(1)=\tbinom31\tfrac18=\tfrac38,\quad p(2)=\tbinom32\tfrac18=\tfrac38,\quad p(3)=\tbinom33\tfrac18=\tfrac18 \]

  2. (b) Mean and standard deviation.

    \[ \mu=np=3(0.5)=\class{dv}{1.5},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{npq}=\sqrt{3(0.5)(0.5)}=\sqrt{0.75}=\class{dv}{0.866} \]

  3. (c) One standard deviation.

    \[ \mu\pm\sigma=1.5\pm0.866=(0.634,\ 2.366) \]

    The integers inside are 1 and 2: \(p(1)+p(2)=\tfrac38+\tfrac38=\class{dv}{0.75}\).

  4. Two standard deviations.

    \[ \mu\pm2\sigma=1.5\pm1.732=(-0.232,\ 3.232) \]

    All of 0, 1, 2, 3 are inside, so the fraction is \(\class{dv}{1}\).

  5. Compare. Empirical rule predicts about 0.68 and 0.95; actual is 0.75 and 1. Tchebysheff guarantees at least 0 and 0.75; both actual values satisfy it.

Spraying cost for diseased trees

WebAssign 3.10.222
Problem

The number of diseased trees per acre, \(Y\), is Poisson with \(\lambda=13\). Spraying costs \(C=5Y+40\) dollars. (a) Find \(E(C)\) and \(\sigma_C\). (b) Find an interval that contains \(C\) with probability at least 0.75.

From the textbook
Poisson mean and variance (Section 3.8)
\(\class{tb}{E(Y)=\lambda,\quad V(Y)=\lambda}\)
Linear functions (Section 3.3)
\(\class{tb}{E(aY+b)=aE(Y)+b,\quad V(aY+b)=a^2V(Y)}\)
Tchebysheff
\(1-1/k^2=0.75\) gives \(k=2\).
Quant financeThis is the expected-cost decision behind credit provisioning: pay for inspection/treatment now versus bear the expected cost of the bad outcome later, the same structure as expected loss \(=\) PD \(\times\) LGD \(\times\) EAD, or the decision to hedge a position versus carry its expected loss on the book.

Solution

  1. Mean of C. Linear functions

    \[ E(C)=5E(Y)+40=5(13)+40=65+40=\class{dv}{105} \]

  2. Variance and standard deviation of C. The \(+40\) shifts the cost but doesn't spread it, so it drops out of the variance.

    \[ V(C)=5^2V(Y)=25(13)=325,\qquad\sigma_C=\sqrt{325}=5\sqrt{13}=\class{dv}{18.028} \]

  3. Find k for probability 0.75.

    \[ 1-\frac1{k^2}=0.75\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac1{k^2}=0.25\ \Longrightarrow\ k^2=4\ \Longrightarrow\ k=2 \]

  4. Build the interval. \(2\sigma_C=2(18.0278)=36.0555\).

    \[ 105\pm36.0555=\class{dv}{(68.94,\ 141.06)} \]

    Live figure A4 shows the actual probability in this interval is about 0.96, much higher than the guaranteed 0.75. Tchebysheff is a worst-case bound.

Poisson approximation for a rare disease

WebAssign 3.SE.501.XP
Problem

A disease occurs at a rate of 50 per 100,000 per year. In a random sample of 1,000 children, let \(Y\) be the number with the disease. (a) Can the distribution of \(Y\) be approximated by a Poisson distribution? If so, with what \(\lambda\)? (b) Find \(P(Y\ge2)\).

From the textbook
Poisson approximation to the binomial (Section 3.8)
When \(n\) is large and \(p\) is small, \(\text{Binomial}(n,p)\approx\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) with \(\class{tb}{\lambda=np}\).
Poisson probability function
\(\class{tb}{p(y)=\dfrac{\lambda^ye^{-\lambda}}{y!}}\)
Complement rule
\(\class{tb}{P(Y\ge2)=1-P(Y=0)-P(Y=1)}\)
Quant financeThe Poisson approximation to a rare binomial event is the standard way quants model rare-but-costly occurrences: operational-risk loss events, defaults scattered through a large retail loan book, or jump arrivals in a jump-diffusion option-pricing model — all cases where \(n\) is huge, \(p\) is tiny, and only \(np\) matters.

Solution

  1. Model. Each child independently has the disease with \(p=\tfrac{50}{100{,}000}=0.0005\). So \(Y\sim\text{Binomial}(1000,\ 0.0005)\).

  2. (a) Check the approximation conditions. \(n=1000\) is large and \(p=0.0005\) is small, so yes.

    \[ \lambda=np=1000(0.0005)=\class{dv}{0.5} \]

  3. (b) Use the complement. "At least 2" has infinitely many terms; its complement has two.

    \[ P(Y=0)=\frac{0.5^0e^{-0.5}}{0!}=e^{-0.5}=0.606531 \] \[ P(Y=1)=\frac{0.5^1e^{-0.5}}{1!}=0.5(0.606531)=0.303265 \]

  4. Subtract.

    \[ P(Y\ge2)=1-(0.606531+0.303265)=1-0.909796=\class{dv}{0.0902} \]

    Exact binomial answer is 0.09017. The approximation is off by about 0.00004 (Live figure A5).

Q8

Clearing inventory in fewer than 6 contacts

WebAssign 3.SE.254
Problem

A heavy-equipment supplier makes a sale with probability 0.25 on each customer contact, independently. There are 4 pieces in inventory. Find the probability that the inventory is sold in fewer than 6 contacts.

From the textbook
Negative binomial distribution (Section 3.6)
\(Y\) = trial on which the \(r\)th success occurs: \(\class{tb}{p(y)=\binom{y-1}{r-1}p^rq^{y-r},\quad y=r,r+1,\dots}\)
Quant finance"Number of trials until the \(r\)th success" distributions are exactly how market-microstructure models count re-quotes until a limit order fills, or margin calls until a position is forced to liquidate — the same waiting-time logic used here for clearing inventory in under 6 contacts.

Solution

  1. Why negative binomial. We count contacts until the 4th sale. That's "trials until the \(r\)th success" with \(r=4\), \(p=0.25\), \(q=0.75\).

  2. Which values count. At least 4 contacts are needed to make 4 sales, so "fewer than 6" means \(Y=4\) or \(Y=5\).

    \[ P(Y\lt6)=p(4)+p(5) \]

  3. p(4). Negative binomial \((0.25)^4=\tfrac1{256}\).

    \[ p(4)=\binom33(0.25)^4(0.75)^0=1\cdot\tfrac1{256}\cdot1=\tfrac1{256}=0.00390625 \]

  4. p(5). \(\binom43=4\).

    \[ p(5)=\binom43(0.25)^4(0.75)^1=4\cdot\tfrac1{256}\cdot\tfrac34=\tfrac{3}{256}=0.01171875 \]

  5. Add.

    \[ P(Y\lt6)=\frac1{256}+\frac3{256}=\frac4{256}=\class{dv}{\frac1{64}=0.015625} \]

Q9

Finding c, F(y), and an area two ways

WebAssign 4.1.003
Problem

\(f(y)=cy\) for \(0\le y\le2\), 0 elsewhere. (a) Find \(c\). (b) Find \(F(y)\). (c) Graph \(f\) and \(F\). (d) Use \(F\) to find \(P(1\le Y\le2)\). (e) Use \(f\) and geometry to find the same probability.

From the textbook
Properties of a density
\(\class{tb}{f(y)\ge0\ \text{and}\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)\,dy=1}\)
Distribution function
\(\class{tb}{F(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}f(t)\,dt}\), and \(\class{tb}{P(a\le Y\le b)=F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^bf(y)\,dy}\)
Quant financeFinding the constant that makes a density integrate to 1 is the same constraint quants solve when calibrating a risk-neutral density from option prices: Breeden–Litzenberger differentiates the call-price curve twice with respect to strike to get a density that must itself integrate to 1 and stay nonnegative — a bad calibration shows up exactly as a normalization or positivity violation, same as here.

Solution

  1. (a) Total area must be 1. Density property

    \[ \int_0^2cy\,dy=c\left[\frac{y^2}2\right]_0^2=c\left(\frac42-0\right)=2c=1\ \Longrightarrow\ \class{dv}{c=\tfrac12} \]

  2. (b) Integrate the density up to y, for \(0\le y\le2\). Use a dummy variable \(t\) so it doesn't clash with the upper limit.

    \[ F(y)=\int_0^y\frac t2\,dt=\left[\frac{t^2}4\right]_0^y=\class{dv}{\frac{y^2}4} \]

    Below 0 there is no area yet, so \(F(y)=0\). Above 2 all the area is used, so \(F(y)=1\).

9.82

Sufficient statistic, MLE and MVUE for a Weibull-type density

Problem

Let \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\) be a random sample from

\[ f(y\mid\theta)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\theta}\,r\,y^{r-1}e^{-y^r/\theta}, & \theta>0,\ y>0,\\[4pt] 0, & \text{elsewhere,}\end{cases} \]

where \(r\) is a known positive constant. (a) Find a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\). (b) Find the MLE of \(\theta\). (c) Is the estimator in (b) an MVUE for \(\theta\)?

From the textbook
Factorization theorem (Section 9.4, Theorem 9.4)
\(U\) is sufficient for \(\theta\) if and only if \(\class{tb}{L(y_1,\dots,y_n\mid\theta)=g(u,\theta)\cdot h(y_1,\dots,y_n)}\), where \(g\) touches the data only through \(u\) and \(h\) does not involve \(\theta\).
Maximum likelihood (Section 9.7)
The MLE is the value of \(\theta\) that maximizes \(L(\theta)\). Because \(\ln\) is increasing, it is the same value that maximizes \(\ln L(\theta)\).
Rao–Blackwell route to the MVUE (Section 9.5)
If \(U\) is a sufficient statistic found by factorization and \(h(U)\) is unbiased for \(\theta\), then \(h(U)\) is the MVUE of \(\theta\). (The book states this result for sufficient statistics found this way; completeness is what makes it airtight, see the note in part c.)
Quant financeWeibull-type densities are the standard choice for time-to-default and time-to-prepayment modeling in credit risk. Finding a sufficient statistic and its MLE is exactly how a hazard-rate / default-intensity model gets calibrated from a loan book's historical survival data, and whether that estimator is an MVUE determines how much sampling noise ends up in a regulatory capital estimate.

Part (a): sufficient statistic

  1. Write the likelihood. The \(Y_i\) are independent with the same density, so the joint density is the product of the marginals.

    \[ L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_i\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{\theta}\,r\,y_i^{r-1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{y_i^r}{\theta}\right)\right] \]

  2. Split the product factor by factor. A product of \(n\) identical constants is a power, and a product of exponentials is the exponential of the sum, \(\prod e^{a_i}=e^{\sum a_i}\).

    \[ \prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\theta}=\theta^{-n},\qquad \prod_{i=1}^n r=r^n,\qquad \prod_{i=1}^n\exp\!\left(-\frac{y_i^r}{\theta}\right)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r\right) \] \[ L(\theta)=\theta^{-n}\,r^n\left(\prod_{i=1}^n y_i^{r-1}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r\right) \]

  3. Group the pieces that contain θ. Let \(u=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r\).

    \[ L(\theta)=\underbrace{\left[\theta^{-n}e^{-u/\theta}\right]}_{g(u,\theta)}\cdot\underbrace{\left[r^n\prod_{i=1}^n y_i^{r-1}\right]}_{h(y_1,\dots,y_n)} \]

  4. Check both conditions of the theorem. \(g(u,\theta)\) depends on the data only through \(u\). \(h\) contains only the data and the known constant \(r\), no \(\theta\). Both factors are non-negative.

    This is why \(r\) has to be known. If \(r\) were a parameter too, \(h\) would contain an unknown and the factorization would be for \(\theta\) only with \(r\) fixed.

Answer (a)

By the factorization theorem, \(\class{dv}{U=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^r}\) is sufficient for \(\theta\).

Part (b): maximum likelihood estimator

  1. Take logs. Use \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\), \(\ln a^k=k\ln a\) and \(\ln e^x=x\), one factor at a time.

    \[ \ln L(\theta)=\ln r^n+\ln\theta^{-n}+\ln\prod_{i=1}^n y_i^{r-1}+\ln\exp\!\left(-\frac{u}{\theta}\right) \] \[ \ln L(\theta)=n\ln r-n\ln\theta+(r-1)\sum_{i=1}^n\ln y_i-\frac{u}{\theta} \]

  2. Differentiate with respect to θ. The first and third terms have no θ, so their derivatives are 0. Use \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta=\frac1\theta\) and \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\theta^{-1}=-\theta^{-2}\).

    \[ \frac{d}{d\theta}\ln L(\theta)=0-\frac{n}{\theta}+0-u\left(-\theta^{-2}\right)=-\frac{n}{\theta}+\frac{u}{\theta^2} \]

  3. Set the derivative to zero and solve.

    \[ -\frac{n}{\hat\theta}+\frac{u}{\hat\theta^{2}}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{u}{\hat\theta^{2}}=\frac{n}{\hat\theta} \]

    Multiply both sides by \(\hat\theta^{2}\) (allowed because \(\hat\theta>0\)):

    \[ u=n\hat\theta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{dv}{\hat\theta=\frac{u}{n}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r} \]

  4. Confirm it is a maximum. Differentiate again:

    \[ \frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}\ln L(\theta)=\frac{n}{\theta^2}-\frac{2u}{\theta^3} \]

    At \(\theta=\hat\theta\), substitute \(u=n\hat\theta\) from step 3:

    \[ \frac{n}{\hat\theta^2}-\frac{2n\hat\theta}{\hat\theta^3}=\frac{n}{\hat\theta^2}-\frac{2n}{\hat\theta^2}=-\frac{n}{\hat\theta^2}\lt 0 \]

    Negative second derivative, and only one critical point on \((0,\infty)\), so \(\hat\theta\) is the global maximum.

Answer (b)

\(\class{dv}{\hat\theta=\dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^r}\). Notice it is a function of the sufficient statistic \(U\) from part (a): \(\hat\theta=U/n\).

Part (c): is it the MVUE?

  1. Use linearity of expectation. Ch. 5, expected value of a linear function

    \[ E(\hat\theta)=E\!\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^r\right)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n E\!\left(Y_i^r\right) \]

    So everything reduces to finding \(E(Y^r)\) for one observation.

  2. Set up the integral for E(Yʳ). Definition of expected value

    \[ E(Y^r)=\int_0^\infty y^r\cdot\frac{r}{\theta}\,y^{r-1}e^{-y^r/\theta}\,dy \]

  3. Substitute. Let \(w=y^r/\theta\). Then \(y^r=\theta w\) and \(dw=\frac{r}{\theta}y^{r-1}\,dy\). When \(y=0\), \(w=0\); as \(y\to\infty\), \(w\to\infty\) (since \(r\gt0\)).

    The integrand groups exactly into these pieces:

    \[ \underbrace{y^r}_{\theta w}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{r}{\theta}y^{r-1}\,dy}_{dw}\cdot\underbrace{e^{-y^r/\theta}}_{e^{-w}} \] \[ E(Y^r)=\int_0^\infty \theta w\,e^{-w}\,dw=\theta\int_0^\infty w^{2-1}e^{-w}\,dw \]

  4. Recognize the gamma function. Gamma function, Section 4.6

    \[ \class{tb}{\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty w^{\alpha-1}e^{-w}\,dw,\qquad \Gamma(n)=(n-1)!} \] \[ E(Y^r)=\theta\,\Gamma(2)=\theta\cdot1!=\theta \]

    Shortcut you can cite on an exam: \(W=Y^r\) has density \(\frac1\theta e^{-w/\theta}\), an exponential with mean \(\theta\). Same conclusion.

  5. Conclude unbiasedness.

    \[ E(\hat\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\theta=\frac1n(n\theta)=\class{dv}{\theta} \]

  6. Apply the Rao–Blackwell result. \(\hat\theta=U/n\) is a function of the sufficient statistic \(U\) found by factorization in part (a), and it is unbiased. So it is the MVUE.

    NoteThe Gemini guide argued through completeness and the Lehmann–Scheffé theorem. That argument is correct (this density is a one-parameter exponential family, so \(U\) is complete), but Wackerly does not develop completeness formally. If your instructor follows the book, write the Rao–Blackwell version above. This is my inference about your course; check your lecture notes.
Answer (c)

Yes. \(\hat\theta=\frac1n\sum Y_i^r\) is unbiased and is a function of the sufficient statistic \(U=\sum Y_i^r\), so it is the MVUE for \(\theta\).

MLE of σ for a normal sample

Problem

\(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) is a random sample from a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), both unknown. Find the MLE of \(\sigma\).

From the textbook
Normal density (Section 4.5)
\(\class{tb}{f(y\mid\mu,\sigma)=\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}\)
Invariance property of MLEs (Section 9.7)
If \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of \(\theta\), then the MLE of \(t(\theta)\) is \(\class{tb}{t(\hat\theta)}\).
MLE of σ² for the normal (worked as an example in Section 9.7)
\(\class{tb}{\hat\mu=\bar Y,\quad \widehat{\sigma^2}=\dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}\)
Quant financeThis is realized-volatility estimation, full stop — the MLE of \(\sigma\) from a sample of returns is the baseline historical-volatility estimator that feeds option pricing, VaR, and GARCH model calibration. Its statistical properties (bias, efficiency) directly determine how noisy the resulting volatility forecast is.

Method 1: invariance (fastest on an exam)

  1. Start from the known MLE of σ². Section 9.7 example

    \[ \class{tb}{\widehat{\sigma^2}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2} \]

  2. Write σ as a function of σ². Let \(t(x)=\sqrt{x}\). Since \(\sigma>0\), \(\sigma=t(\sigma^2)=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\).

  3. Apply invariance. Invariance property

    \[ \hat\sigma=t\!\left(\widehat{\sigma^2}\right)=\class{dv}{\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}} \]

Method 2: derive it directly

  1. Likelihood. Product of \(n\) normal densities; combine the constants into powers and the exponentials into one exponent.

    \[ L(\mu,\sigma)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(y_i-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\sigma^{-n}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2\right) \]

  2. Log-likelihood.

    \[ \ln L(\mu,\sigma)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi)-n\ln\sigma-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2 \]

  3. Partial derivative in μ. Chain rule: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(y_i-\mu)^2=2(y_i-\mu)(-1)\).

    \[ \frac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\mu}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n 2(y_i-\mu)(-1)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu) \]

    Set to 0 and multiply by \(\sigma^2\):

    \[ \sum_{i=1}^n y_i-n\hat\mu=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{\hat\mu=\bar y} \]

  4. Partial derivative in σ. Write the last term as \(-\tfrac12\sigma^{-2}\sum(y_i-\mu)^2\); its derivative is \(-\tfrac12(-2)\sigma^{-3}\sum(y_i-\mu)^2\).

    \[ \frac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac n\sigma+\frac{1}{\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2 \]

  5. Set to 0 with μ̂ = ȳ substituted, and solve.

    \[ -\frac{n}{\hat\sigma}+\frac{1}{\hat\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{n}{\hat\sigma}=\frac{1}{\hat\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2 \]

    Multiply both sides by \(\hat\sigma^3\), divide by \(n\), take the positive root:

    \[ n\hat\sigma^2=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2}} \]

  6. Why this is a maximum. Fix \(\mu=\bar y\) and let \(S=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\gt0\). As \(\sigma\to0^+\), the term \(-S/(2\sigma^2)\to-\infty\). As \(\sigma\to\infty\), \(-n\ln\sigma\to-\infty\). A function that goes to \(-\infty\) at both ends with one critical point must peak there. Live figure B4 shows exactly this shape.

Answer

\(\class{dv}{\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}}\). The divisor is \(n\), not \(n-1\).

Matching α for two tests on uniform data

Problem

\(Y_1\) and \(Y_2\) are independent and uniform on \((\theta,\theta+1)\). To test \(H_0:\theta=0\) against \(H_a:\theta\gt0\), Test 1 rejects when \(Y_1\gt0.95\) and Test 2 rejects when \(Y_1+Y_2\gt c\). Find \(c\) so that Test 2 has the same α as Test 1. (Hint: Example 6.3 gives the distribution of the sum of two independent uniform(0,1) variables.)

From the textbook
Type I error probability (Section 10.2)
\(\class{tb}{\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is true})}\)
Uniform distribution (Section 4.4)
If \(Y\sim U(a,b)\), then \(\class{tb}{P(Y\le y)=\dfrac{y-a}{b-a}}\) for \(a\le y\le b\).
Sum of two independent U(0,1) variables (Example 6.3)
\(\class{tb}{F_U(u)=\begin{cases}0,&u\lt0\\ u^2/2,&0\le u\le1\\ 1-(2-u)^2/2,&1\lt u\le2\\ 1,&u\gt2\end{cases}}\)
Quant financeMatching the Type-I error rate between two different test statistics is exactly what you do when comparing two VaR-breach backtests (e.g. Kupiec's POF test vs. a conditional-coverage test) or two trading-signal screens — they need to be calibrated to the same false-positive rate before their power can be compared.

Solution

  1. State what H₀ means for the data. α is computed assuming \(H_0\) is true, so set \(\theta=0\): \(Y_1,Y_2\) are independent \(U(0,1)\).

  2. α for Test 1. Uniform CDF

    \[ \alpha_1=P(Y_1\gt0.95\mid\theta=0)=1-P(Y_1\le0.95)=1-\frac{0.95-0}{1-0}=\class{dv}{0.05} \]

  3. Where the CDF of U = Y₁ + Y₂ comes from. \((Y_1,Y_2)\) is uniform on the unit square, so \(P(U\le u)\) is the area of the square below the line \(y_1+y_2=u\).

    For \(0\le u\le1\) that region is a triangle with legs \(u\): area \(\tfrac12u\cdot u=u^2/2\).

    For \(1\lt u\le2\) the region missing is a triangle in the top-right corner with legs \(2-u\): area \(1-\tfrac12(2-u)^2\).

    This matches Example 6.3. Differentiating gives the triangular density \(f_U(u)=u\) on \([0,1]\) and \(2-u\) on \((1,2]\), drawn in Live figure B6.

  4. Write α for Test 2 and set it equal to α₁.

    \[ \alpha_2=P(U\gt c\mid\theta=0)=1-F_U(c)=0.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_U(c)=0.95 \]

  5. Decide which piece of the CDF to use. \(F_U(1)=1^2/2=0.50\lt0.95\), so \(c\) must lie in \((1,2]\).

  6. Solve.

    \[ 1-\frac{(2-c)^2}{2}=0.95 \]

    Subtract 1 from both sides and multiply by −1:

    \[ \frac{(2-c)^2}{2}=0.05 \]

    Multiply both sides by 2:

    \[ (2-c)^2=0.10 \]

    Take square roots. \(2-c=\pm\sqrt{0.10}\), and we keep the positive root because \(c\lt2\):

    \[ 2-c=\sqrt{0.10}=0.316228\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{dv}{c=2-\sqrt{0.10}\approx1.6838} \]

  7. Check by plugging back in.

    \[ \alpha_2=\frac{(2-1.683772)^2}{2}=\frac{(0.316228)^2}{2}=\frac{0.100000}{2}=0.05\ \checkmark \]
Answer

\(\class{dv}{c=2-\sqrt{0.10}\approx1.684}\). Both tests then have \(\alpha=0.05\).

Binding constraints and the price of a resource

HW7 · Problem 1
Problem

Maximize \(Z=x_1+2x_2\) subject to \(x_1+3x_2\le8\) (resource 1), \(x_1+x_2\le4\) (resource 2), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\). The optimal solution is \((x_1^*,x_2^*)=(2,2)\) with \(Z^*=6\).

(a) Is either constraint binding? (b) Use graphical analysis to find the shadow price of each resource. (c) How many additional units of resource 1 would raise the optimal value by 1.5?

From the textbook
Binding constraint
A constraint that holds with equality at the optimal solution. A nonbinding constraint has slack left over, so a little more of that resource changes nothing: its shadow price is 0.
Shadow price
\(\class{tb}{y_i^*=\dfrac{\Delta Z^*}{\Delta b_i}}\): the rate at which the optimal value rises per extra unit of resource \(i\), all else fixed. It holds while the same constraints stay binding (the allowable range).
Graphical method
Shift the resource's constraint line out by one unit, find the new corner where the binding lines cross, and measure the change in \(Z\).
Quant financeShadow prices from a linear program are the marginal value of one more unit of a constrained resource — in portfolio optimization this is literally the marginal utility of one more dollar of risk budget or capital, the number that tells a desk exactly how much performance it's giving up because of a capital constraint.

Solution

  1. (a) Plug the optimum into each constraint.

    \[ x_1+3x_2=2+3(2)=8=8,\qquad x_1+x_2=2+2=4=4 \]

    Both hold with equality, so \(\class{dv}{\text{both constraints are binding}}\).

  2. Keep the right-hand sides as symbols. The optimal corner is where the two binding lines cross, so solve them with \(b_1,b_2\) in place of 8 and 4.

    \[ x_1+3x_2=b_1,\qquad x_1+x_2=b_2 \]

    Subtract the second from the first: \(2x_2=b_1-b_2\), so \(x_2=\dfrac{b_1-b_2}{2}\). Then \(x_1=b_2-x_2=\dfrac{3b_2-b_1}{2}\).

  3. Write the optimal value as a function of the resources.

    \[ Z^*=x_1+2x_2=\frac{3b_2-b_1}{2}+(b_1-b_2)=\frac{3b_2-b_1+2b_1-2b_2}{2}=\frac{b_1+b_2}{2} \]

  4. (b) Read off the shadow prices. Shadow price Each extra unit of either resource adds one half to \(Z^*\).

    \[ \class{dv}{y_1^*=\tfrac12,\qquad y_2^*=\tfrac12} \]

    Graphical check: with \(b_1=9\) the corner moves to \((1.5,\,2.5)\) and \(Z=1.5+5=6.5\). With \(b_2=5\) it moves to \((3.5,\,1.5)\) and \(Z=3.5+3=6.5\). Both are \(+0.5\).

  5. Find how far \(b_1\) can move with the same binding pair (keep \(b_2=4\)). Both coordinates of the corner must stay nonnegative.

    \[ x_1=\frac{12-b_1}{2}\ge0\Rightarrow b_1\le12,\qquad x_2=\frac{b_1-4}{2}\ge0\Rightarrow b_1\ge4 \]

    So the shadow price \(\tfrac12\) holds for \(4\le b_1\le12\).

  6. (c) Solve for the extra units.

    \[ \Delta Z^*=y_1^*\,\Delta b_1\ \Longrightarrow\ 1.5=\tfrac12\,\Delta b_1\ \Longrightarrow\ \class{dv}{\Delta b_1=3} \]

    New \(b_1=11\) is inside \([4,12]\), so the shadow price still applies. Check: the corner becomes \((0.5,\,3.5)\) and \(Z=0.5+7=7.5=6+1.5\ \checkmark\)

Answer
(a)Both constraints are binding
(b)\(y_1^*=\) 0.5, \(y_2^*=\) 0.5
(c)3 additional units of resource 1
OR2

How far can the objective tilt?

HW7 · Problem 2
Problem

Same constraints as OR1. Use sensitivity analysis on the objective coefficients. (a) If \(Z=21x_1+60x_2\), does \((2,2)\) stay optimal? (b) If \(Z=11x_1+10x_2\)? (c) With \(c_1=1\) fixed, what range of \(c_2\) keeps \((2,2)\) optimal? (d) With \(c_2=2\) fixed, what range of \(c_1\)?

From the textbook
Slope test for a corner
The iso-profit line \(c_1x_1+c_2x_2=Z\) has slope \(\class{tb}{-c_1/c_2}\). A corner stays optimal while that slope stays between the slopes of the two constraint lines that meet there.
Same test, as a cone
Equivalently, \((c_1,c_2)\) must lie between the normal vectors of the two binding constraints. Live figure C2 draws that cone.
Quant financeAsking how far objective coefficients can move before the optimal corner changes is objective-sensitivity analysis — in a mean-variance portfolio, it tells you how wrong your expected-return forecasts can be before the optimizer would have picked a different portfolio, exactly the robustness check real portfolio construction needs.

Solution

  1. Slopes of the binding lines at \((2,2)\).

    \[ x_1+3x_2=8\ \Rightarrow\ x_2=\tfrac83-\tfrac13x_1\ (\text{slope }-\tfrac13),\qquad x_1+x_2=4\ \Rightarrow\ \text{slope }-1 \]

  2. Optimality condition. Slope test With \(c_2\gt0\):

    \[ -1\le-\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le-\frac13\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{\frac13\le\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le1} \]

    Current objective: \(c_1/c_2=1/2\), inside the range, which is why \((2,2)\) is optimal now.

  3. (a) \(c_1/c_2=21/60=0.35\). Since \(0.333\le0.35\le1\), \(\class{dv}{\text{yes, }(2,2)\text{ stays optimal}}\).

    Check the corners: \((2,2)\to162\), \((0,\tfrac83)\to160\), \((4,0)\to84\). Close, but \((2,2)\) wins.

  4. (b) \(c_1/c_2=11/10=1.1\gt1\). \(\class{dv}{\text{No.}}\) The new optimum is \((4,0)\): \(Z=44\) beats \((2,2)\)'s 42.

  5. (c) Set \(c_1=1\): \(\tfrac13\le\tfrac1{c_2}\le1\). Taking reciprocals of positive numbers flips the inequalities:

    \[ \class{dv}{1\le c_2\le3} \]

  6. (d) Set \(c_2=2\): \(\tfrac13\le\tfrac{c_1}{2}\le1\). Multiply by 2:

    \[ \class{dv}{\tfrac23\le c_1\le2} \]

    At an endpoint the iso-profit line lies flat along an edge, so a second corner ties; \((2,2)\) is still optimal, which is why the endpoints are included.

Answer
(a)Yes (ratio 0.35 is in [1/3, 1])
(b)No (ratio 1.1; new optimum (4, 0))
(c)1 ≤ c₂ ≤ 3
(d)2/3 ≤ c₁ ≤ 2
Problems 3 and 4 · the three-resource model
OR3

Three resources, one with slack

HW7 · Problem 3
Problem

Maximize \(Z=3x_1+2x_2\) subject to \(x_1\le4\) (resource 1), \(x_1+3x_2\le15\) (resource 2), \(2x_1+x_2\le10\) (resource 3), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\). The optimal solution is \((3,4)\) with \(Z^*=17\).

(a) Which constraints are binding? (b) Use graphical analysis to find the shadow prices.

From the textbook
Nonbinding means a zero price
\(\class{tb}{\text{slack}_i\gt0\ \Rightarrow\ y_i^*=0}\). Small changes to that resource leave the optimal corner where it is.
Shadow price
\(\class{tb}{y_i^*=\Delta Z^*/\Delta b_i}\) within the allowable range, found by re-solving the binding system with \(b_i\) as a symbol.
Quant financeA resource with slack (shadow price zero) is a constraint that isn't actually binding — recognizing this is how a portfolio manager knows which risk or capital limits are truly constraining the portfolio versus which ones have headroom and don't matter yet.

Solution

  1. (a) Plug \((3,4)\) into each constraint.

    \[ x_1=3\lt4\ (\text{slack }1),\qquad x_1+3x_2=3+12=15,\qquad 2x_1+x_2=6+4=10 \]

    \(\class{dv}{\text{Resources 2 and 3 are binding; resource 1 is not.}}\)

  2. Resource 1. It has one unused unit, so \(\class{dv}{y_1^*=0}\).

  3. Solve the binding pair with symbols.

    \[ x_1+3x_2=b_2,\qquad 2x_1+x_2=b_3 \]

    From the second, \(x_2=b_3-2x_1\). Substitute: \(x_1+3b_3-6x_1=b_2\), so \(x_1=\dfrac{3b_3-b_2}{5}\) and \(x_2=b_3-2x_1=\dfrac{2b_2-b_3}{5}\).

  4. Optimal value as a function of \(b_2,b_3\).

    \[ Z^*=3x_1+2x_2=\frac{9b_3-3b_2+4b_2-2b_3}{5}=\frac{b_2+7b_3}{5} \] \[ \class{dv}{y_2^*=\tfrac15=0.2,\qquad y_3^*=\tfrac75=1.4} \]

  5. Graphical check. \(b_2=16\): corner \((2.8,\,4.4)\), \(Z=8.4+8.8=17.2\), up 0.2. \(b_3=11\): corner \((3.6,\,3.8)\), \(Z=10.8+7.6=18.4\), up 1.4, and \(x_1=3.6\le4\) still holds.

    The statement lists \(x_3\ge0\), but this problem has no \(x_3\). It's a typo; ignore it.

Answer
(a)Resources 2 and 3 binding; resource 1 not binding
(b)\(y_1^*=\) 0, \(y_2^*=\) 0.2, \(y_3^*=\) 1.4
OR4

Which corner wins for every objective

HW7 · Problem 4
Problem

Same constraints as OR3, with objective \(Z=c_1x_1+c_2x_2\), \(c_1,c_2\ge0\). Find the range of \(c_1/c_2\) that keeps each point optimal: (a) \((3,4)\), (b) \((4,2)\), (c) \((0,5)\). (d) If \(c_1,c_2\) may be negative, find the conditions on \(c_1,c_2\) and the range of \(c_1/c_2\) that keep \((4,0)\) optimal.

From the textbook
Slope test at each corner
Corner optimal \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\class{tb}{-c_1/c_2}\) lies between the slopes of the two lines that meet there.
Cone test (works for any signs)
Write each constraint as \(a_1x_1+a_2x_2\le b\), so \(x_1\ge0\) becomes \(-x_1\le0\). A corner is optimal \(\Leftrightarrow\) \((c_1,c_2)\) is a nonnegative combination of the normals \((a_1,a_2)\) of the constraints active there.
Quant financeMapping which vertex is optimal across a whole range of objective directions is exactly how an efficient frontier is built: each vertex of the feasible region is optimal for some range of risk-aversion levels, the same way a corner point here is optimal for a range of cost ratios.

Solution

  1. List the corners and the lines meeting at each. Going around the region: \((0,0)\), \((4,0)\), \((4,2)\), \((3,4)\), \((0,5)\).

    \((4,2)\): \(x_1=4\) (vertical) and \(2x_1+x_2=10\) (slope \(-2\)). \((3,4)\): slope \(-2\) and \(x_1+3x_2=15\) (slope \(-\tfrac13\)). \((0,5)\): slope \(-\tfrac13\) and \(x_1=0\) (vertical).

  2. (a) \((3,4)\). Objective slope between \(-2\) and \(-\tfrac13\):

    \[ -2\le-\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le-\frac13\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \class{dv}{\frac13\le\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le2} \]

    The original \(Z=3x_1+2x_2\) has ratio 1.5, inside, consistent with \((3,4)\) being optimal.

  3. (b) \((4,2)\). Slope between vertical and \(-2\), so steeper than \(-2\):

    \[ -\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le-2\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \class{dv}{\frac{c_1}{c_2}\ge2} \]

    If \(c_2=0\) the ratio is undefined; then the whole edge \(x_1=4\) is optimal, so \((4,2)\) still is, tied with \((4,0)\).

  4. (c) \((0,5)\). Slope between \(-\tfrac13\) and 0 (with \(c_1\ge0\)):

    \[ -\frac13\le-\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \class{dv}{0\le\frac{c_1}{c_2}\le\frac13} \]

  5. (d) \((4,0)\) with any signs. Cone test Active constraints there: \(x_1\le4\) with normal \((1,0)\), and \(x_2\ge0\), written \(-x_2\le0\), with normal \((0,-1)\).

    \[ (c_1,c_2)=\lambda(1,0)+\mu(0,-1)=(\lambda,-\mu),\ \lambda,\mu\ge0\ \Longrightarrow\ \class{dv}{c_1\ge0,\ c_2\le0} \]

    For \(c_2\lt0\) that gives \(\class{dv}{c_1/c_2\le0}\).

  6. Why the ratio alone isn't enough in (d). \(c_1/c_2\le0\) also holds for \(c_1=-1,\ c_2=1\), but then \(Z=-x_1+x_2\) is maximized at \((0,5)\), not \((4,0)\). The signs carry information the ratio throws away.

    Check with \(c=(1,-1)\): corners give \(0,\ 4,\ 2,\ -1,\ -5\), so \((4,0)\) wins.

Answer
(a)1/3 ≤ c₁/c₂ ≤ 2
(b)c₁/c₂ ≥ 2
(c)0 ≤ c₁/c₂ ≤ 1/3
(d)c₁ ≥ 0 and c₂ ≤ 0 (not both 0); for c₂ < 0, c₁/c₂ ≤ 0

(a) What is the shadow price of each resource? (b) If every capacity could be increased, which resource has priority? (c) Z is profit in millions, and one more unit of any resource costs $1 million. Should you buy one more unit of resource 1? Resource 2? Resource 3?

From the textbook
Shadow prices in the final tableau
With slack variables \(x_4,x_5,x_6\) added to constraints 1, 2, 3: \(\class{tb}{y_i^*=\text{coefficient of slack }i\text{ in row 0}}\).
Allowable range for \(b_i\)
The slack columns of the final tableau form \(B^{-1}\). Changing \(b_i\) by \(\Delta\) changes the basic variables to \(\class{tb}{x_B+\Delta\cdot(\text{column of slack }i)}\). The shadow price holds while all of them stay \(\ge0\).
Quant financeReading shadow prices and their allowable ranges straight off the final simplex tableau is how a risk desk gets both the price of relaxing a limit and the range over which that price stays valid — beyond the allowable range the whole optimal portfolio restructures, not just its P&L.

Solution

  1. Match slacks to resources. \(x_4\) is the slack of resource 1, \(x_5\) of resource 2, \(x_6\) of resource 3.

  2. (a) Read row 0 under the slack columns. Final tableau rule

    \[ \class{dv}{y_1^*=0,\qquad y_2^*=1.5,\qquad y_3^*=0.5} \]

    Consistency: \(x_4=1\) is basic, so resource 1 has one unused unit, which is exactly why its price is 0.

  3. Cross-check against the original problem. The tableau's solution is \(x_1=1.5,\ x_2=0.5,\ x_3=0\):

    \[ Z=2(1.5)-0.5+0=2.5,\qquad 3(1.5)+0.5=5\le6,\qquad 1.5-0.5=1,\qquad 1.5+0.5=2 \]

    Dual check: \(6y_1^*+1y_2^*+2y_3^*=0+1.5+1=2.5=Z^*\ \checkmark\)

  4. (b) Priority. The largest shadow price wins: \(\class{dv}{\text{resource 2}}\), worth 1.5 per unit, three times resource 3.

  5. Allowable ranges. Current \(x_B=(x_4,x_1,x_2)=(1,\,1.5,\,0.5)\). Slack columns: \(x_5\to(-1,\,0.5,\,-0.5)\), \(x_6\to(-2,\,0.5,\,0.5)\).

    \[ b_2+\Delta:\ (1-\Delta,\ 1.5+0.5\Delta,\ 0.5-0.5\Delta)\ge0\ \Rightarrow\ -3\le\Delta\le1 \] \[ b_3+\Delta:\ (1-2\Delta,\ 1.5+0.5\Delta,\ 0.5+0.5\Delta)\ge0\ \Rightarrow\ -1\le\Delta\le0.5 \]

  6. (c) Compare each price with the $1 million cost.

    Resource 1: \(0\lt1\). \(\class{dv}{\text{Don't buy}}\); it already has an unused unit.

    Resource 2: \(1.5\gt1\), and \(\Delta=1\) is inside \([-3,1]\). \(\class{dv}{\text{Buy}}\): net gain \(1.5-1=0.5\) million. The new optimum is \((2,0,0)\) with \(Z=4\).

    Resource 3: \(0.5\lt1\). \(\class{dv}{\text{Don't buy}}\). It's worse than it looks: \(\Delta=1\) is past the allowable 0.5, so the real gain is only 0.25 (\(Z\) goes to 2.75), a net loss of 0.75 million.

    Live figure C5 shows all three. Inside the allowable range \(Z^*\) follows the shadow-price line exactly; past it, the curve bends down.

Answer
(a)\(y_1^*=\) 0, \(y_2^*=\) 1.5, \(y_3^*=\) 0.5
(b)Resource 2
(c)Resource 1: no · Resource 2: yes, +0.5 million · Resource 3: no
