9.82

Sufficient statistic, MLE and MVUE for a Weibull-type density

Problem

Let \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n\) be a random sample from

\[ f(y\mid\theta)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\theta}\,r\,y^{r-1}e^{-y^r/\theta}, & \theta>0,\ y>0,\\[4pt] 0, & \text{elsewhere,}\end{cases} \]

where \(r\) is a known positive constant. (a) Find a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\). (b) Find the MLE of \(\theta\). (c) Is the estimator in (b) an MVUE for \(\theta\)?

From the textbook
Factorization theorem (Section 9.4, Theorem 9.4)
\(U\) is sufficient for \(\theta\) if and only if \(\class{tb}{L(y_1,\dots,y_n\mid\theta)=g(u,\theta)\cdot h(y_1,\dots,y_n)}\), where \(g\) touches the data only through \(u\) and \(h\) does not involve \(\theta\).
Maximum likelihood (Section 9.7)
The MLE is the value of \(\theta\) that maximizes \(L(\theta)\). Because \(\ln\) is increasing, it is the same value that maximizes \(\ln L(\theta)\).
Rao–Blackwell route to the MVUE (Section 9.5)
If \(U\) is a sufficient statistic found by factorization and \(h(U)\) is unbiased for \(\theta\), then \(h(U)\) is the MVUE of \(\theta\). (The book states this result for sufficient statistics found this way; completeness is what makes it airtight, see the note in part c.)
⚡ Credit Risk & Fixed Income: Hazard Rates & Default Intensity Credit Default Swaps & Basel IRB
Weibull and Generalized Gamma densities are the Wall Street standard for time-to-default and early mortgage prepayment modeling in structured credit (MBS, CLO, and CDS pricing). Finding the sufficient statistic and MLE is how hazard rates are calibrated from corporate default datasets. Because the sufficient statistic \(U = \sum Y_i^r\) condenses all sample information without loss, the estimator achieves the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (MVUE), minimizing sampling variance in regulatory Basel Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) capital.
Wall Street Quant Interview (PIMCO / BlackRock / Goldman Fixed Income): "What is the economic difference between a constant hazard rate (Exponential) and a time-varying hazard rate (Weibull with shape parameter \(r\)) in credit modeling?"
Desk Takeaway: If \(r = 1\), the hazard rate is constant (memoryless Poisson default), meaning a firm that has survived 10 years has the exact same default probability tomorrow as a brand new startup. If \(r > 1\), the hazard rate increases with time (aging/wear-out, credit rating degradation). If \(r < 1\), the hazard rate decreases ('infant mortality'), typical of tech startups where surviving the first 2 years drastically lowers future failure probability.

Part (a): sufficient statistic

  1. Write the likelihood. The \(Y_i\) are independent with the same density, so the joint density is the product of the marginals.

    \[ L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(y_i\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{\theta}\,r\,y_i^{r-1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{y_i^r}{\theta}\right)\right] \]

  2. Split the product factor by factor. A product of \(n\) identical constants is a power, and a product of exponentials is the exponential of the sum, \(\prod e^{a_i}=e^{\sum a_i}\).

    \[ \prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\theta}=\theta^{-n},\qquad \prod_{i=1}^n r=r^n,\qquad \prod_{i=1}^n\exp\!\left(-\frac{y_i^r}{\theta}\right)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r\right) \] \[ L(\theta)=\theta^{-n}\,r^n\left(\prod_{i=1}^n y_i^{r-1}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r\right) \]

  3. Group the pieces that contain θ. Let \(u=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r\).

    \[ L(\theta)=\underbrace{\left[\theta^{-n}e^{-u/\theta}\right]}_{g(u,\theta)}\cdot\underbrace{\left[r^n\prod_{i=1}^n y_i^{r-1}\right]}_{h(y_1,\dots,y_n)} \]

  4. Check both conditions of the theorem. \(g(u,\theta)\) depends on the data only through \(u\). \(h\) contains only the data and the known constant \(r\), no \(\theta\). Both factors are non-negative.

    This is why \(r\) has to be known. If \(r\) were a parameter too, \(h\) would contain an unknown and the factorization would be for \(\theta\) only with \(r\) fixed.

Answer (a)

By the factorization theorem, \(\class{dv}{U=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^r}\) is sufficient for \(\theta\).

Part (b): maximum likelihood estimator

  1. Take logs. Use \(\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b\), \(\ln a^k=k\ln a\) and \(\ln e^x=x\), one factor at a time.

    \[ \ln L(\theta)=\ln r^n+\ln\theta^{-n}+\ln\prod_{i=1}^n y_i^{r-1}+\ln\exp\!\left(-\frac{u}{\theta}\right) \] \[ \ln L(\theta)=n\ln r-n\ln\theta+(r-1)\sum_{i=1}^n\ln y_i-\frac{u}{\theta} \]

  2. Differentiate with respect to θ. The first and third terms have no θ, so their derivatives are 0. Use \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta=\frac1\theta\) and \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\theta^{-1}=-\theta^{-2}\).

    \[ \frac{d}{d\theta}\ln L(\theta)=0-\frac{n}{\theta}+0-u\left(-\theta^{-2}\right)=-\frac{n}{\theta}+\frac{u}{\theta^2} \]

  3. Set the derivative to zero and solve.

    \[ -\frac{n}{\hat\theta}+\frac{u}{\hat\theta^{2}}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{u}{\hat\theta^{2}}=\frac{n}{\hat\theta} \]

    Multiply both sides by \(\hat\theta^{2}\) (allowed because \(\hat\theta>0\)):

    \[ u=n\hat\theta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{dv}{\hat\theta=\frac{u}{n}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i^r} \]

  4. Confirm it is a maximum. Differentiate again:

    \[ \frac{d^2}{d\theta^2}\ln L(\theta)=\frac{n}{\theta^2}-\frac{2u}{\theta^3} \]

    At \(\theta=\hat\theta\), substitute \(u=n\hat\theta\) from step 3:

    \[ \frac{n}{\hat\theta^2}-\frac{2n\hat\theta}{\hat\theta^3}=\frac{n}{\hat\theta^2}-\frac{2n}{\hat\theta^2}=-\frac{n}{\hat\theta^2}\lt 0 \]

    Negative second derivative, and only one critical point on \((0,\infty)\), so \(\hat\theta\) is the global maximum.

Answer (b)

\(\class{dv}{\hat\theta=\dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^r}\). Notice it is a function of the sufficient statistic \(U\) from part (a): \(\hat\theta=U/n\).

Part (c): is it the MVUE?

  1. Use linearity of expectation. Ch. 5, expected value of a linear function

    \[ E(\hat\theta)=E\!\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^r\right)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n E\!\left(Y_i^r\right) \]

    So everything reduces to finding \(E(Y^r)\) for one observation.

  2. Set up the integral for E(Yʳ). Definition of expected value

    \[ E(Y^r)=\int_0^\infty y^r\cdot\frac{r}{\theta}\,y^{r-1}e^{-y^r/\theta}\,dy \]

  3. Substitute. Let \(w=y^r/\theta\). Then \(y^r=\theta w\) and \(dw=\frac{r}{\theta}y^{r-1}\,dy\). When \(y=0\), \(w=0\); as \(y\to\infty\), \(w\to\infty\) (since \(r\gt0\)).

    The integrand groups exactly into these pieces:

    \[ \underbrace{y^r}_{\theta w}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{r}{\theta}y^{r-1}\,dy}_{dw}\cdot\underbrace{e^{-y^r/\theta}}_{e^{-w}} \] \[ E(Y^r)=\int_0^\infty \theta w\,e^{-w}\,dw=\theta\int_0^\infty w^{2-1}e^{-w}\,dw \]

  4. Recognize the gamma function. Gamma function, Section 4.6

    \[ \class{tb}{\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty w^{\alpha-1}e^{-w}\,dw,\qquad \Gamma(n)=(n-1)!} \] \[ E(Y^r)=\theta\,\Gamma(2)=\theta\cdot1!=\theta \]

    Shortcut you can cite on an exam: \(W=Y^r\) has density \(\frac1\theta e^{-w/\theta}\), an exponential with mean \(\theta\). Same conclusion.

  5. Conclude unbiasedness.

    \[ E(\hat\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\theta=\frac1n(n\theta)=\class{dv}{\theta} \]

  6. Apply the Rao–Blackwell result. \(\hat\theta=U/n\) is a function of the sufficient statistic \(U\) found by factorization in part (a), and it is unbiased. So it is the MVUE.

    NoteThe Gemini guide argued through completeness and the Lehmann–Scheffé theorem. That argument is correct (this density is a one-parameter exponential family, so \(U\) is complete), but Wackerly does not develop completeness formally. If your instructor follows the book, write the Rao–Blackwell version above. This is my inference about your course; check your lecture notes.
Answer (c)

Yes. \(\hat\theta=\frac1n\sum Y_i^r\) is unbiased and is a function of the sufficient statistic \(U=\sum Y_i^r\), so it is the MVUE for \(\theta\).

9.96

MLE of σ for a normal sample

Problem

\(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) is a random sample from a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), both unknown. Find the MLE of \(\sigma\).

From the textbook
Normal density (Section 4.5)
\(\class{tb}{f(y\mid\mu,\sigma)=\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}\)
Invariance property of MLEs (Section 9.7)
If \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of \(\theta\), then the MLE of \(t(\theta)\) is \(\class{tb}{t(\hat\theta)}\).
MLE of σ² for the normal (worked as an example in Section 9.7)
\(\class{tb}{\hat\mu=\bar Y,\quad \widehat{\sigma^2}=\dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}\)
Quant financeThis is realized-volatility estimation, full stop — the MLE of \(\sigma\) from a sample of returns is the baseline historical-volatility estimator that feeds option pricing, VaR, and GARCH model calibration. Its statistical properties (bias, efficiency) directly determine how noisy the resulting volatility forecast is.

Method 1: invariance (fastest on an exam)

  1. Start from the known MLE of σ². Section 9.7 example

    \[ \class{tb}{\widehat{\sigma^2}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2} \]

  2. Write σ as a function of σ². Let \(t(x)=\sqrt{x}\). Since \(\sigma>0\), \(\sigma=t(\sigma^2)=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\).

  3. Apply invariance. Invariance property

    \[ \hat\sigma=t\!\left(\widehat{\sigma^2}\right)=\class{dv}{\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}} \]

Method 2: derive it directly

  1. Likelihood. Product of \(n\) normal densities; combine the constants into powers and the exponentials into one exponent.

    \[ L(\mu,\sigma)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(y_i-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\sigma^{-n}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2\right) \]

  2. Log-likelihood.

    \[ \ln L(\mu,\sigma)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi)-n\ln\sigma-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2 \]

  3. Partial derivative in μ. Chain rule: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(y_i-\mu)^2=2(y_i-\mu)(-1)\).

    \[ \frac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\mu}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n 2(y_i-\mu)(-1)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu) \]

    Set to 0 and multiply by \(\sigma^2\):

    \[ \sum_{i=1}^n y_i-n\hat\mu=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{\hat\mu=\bar y} \]

  4. Partial derivative in σ. Write the last term as \(-\tfrac12\sigma^{-2}\sum(y_i-\mu)^2\); its derivative is \(-\tfrac12(-2)\sigma^{-3}\sum(y_i-\mu)^2\).

    \[ \frac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac n\sigma+\frac{1}{\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2 \]

  5. Set to 0 with μ̂ = ȳ substituted, and solve.

    \[ -\frac{n}{\hat\sigma}+\frac{1}{\hat\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{n}{\hat\sigma}=\frac{1}{\hat\sigma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2 \]

    Multiply both sides by \(\hat\sigma^3\), divide by \(n\), take the positive root:

    \[ n\hat\sigma^2=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\class{dv}{\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2}} \]

  6. Why this is a maximum. Fix \(\mu=\bar y\) and let \(S=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\gt0\). As \(\sigma\to0^+\), the term \(-S/(2\sigma^2)\to-\infty\). As \(\sigma\to\infty\), \(-n\ln\sigma\to-\infty\). A function that goes to \(-\infty\) at both ends with one critical point must peak there. Live figure B4 shows exactly this shape.

Answer

\(\class{dv}{\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}}\). The divisor is \(n\), not \(n-1\).

10.5

Matching α for two tests on uniform data

Problem

\(Y_1\) and \(Y_2\) are independent and uniform on \((\theta,\theta+1)\). To test \(H_0:\theta=0\) against \(H_a:\theta\gt0\), Test 1 rejects when \(Y_1\gt0.95\) and Test 2 rejects when \(Y_1+Y_2\gt c\). Find \(c\) so that Test 2 has the same α as Test 1. (Hint: Example 6.3 gives the distribution of the sum of two independent uniform(0,1) variables.)

From the textbook
Type I error probability (Section 10.2)
\(\class{tb}{\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is true})}\)
Uniform distribution (Section 4.4)
If \(Y\sim U(a,b)\), then \(\class{tb}{P(Y\le y)=\dfrac{y-a}{b-a}}\) for \(a\le y\le b\).
Sum of two independent U(0,1) variables (Example 6.3)
\(\class{tb}{F_U(u)=\begin{cases}0,&u\lt0\\ u^2/2,&0\le u\le1\\ 1-(2-u)^2/2,&1\lt u\le2\\ 1,&u\gt2\end{cases}}\)
Quant financeMatching the Type-I error rate between two different test statistics is exactly what you do when comparing two VaR-breach backtests (e.g. Kupiec's POF test vs. a conditional-coverage test) or two trading-signal screens — they need to be calibrated to the same false-positive rate before their power can be compared.

Solution

  1. State what H₀ means for the data. α is computed assuming \(H_0\) is true, so set \(\theta=0\): \(Y_1,Y_2\) are independent \(U(0,1)\).

  2. α for Test 1. Uniform CDF

    \[ \alpha_1=P(Y_1\gt0.95\mid\theta=0)=1-P(Y_1\le0.95)=1-\frac{0.95-0}{1-0}=\class{dv}{0.05} \]

  3. Where the CDF of U = Y₁ + Y₂ comes from. \((Y_1,Y_2)\) is uniform on the unit square, so \(P(U\le u)\) is the area of the square below the line \(y_1+y_2=u\).

    For \(0\le u\le1\) that region is a triangle with legs \(u\): area \(\tfrac12u\cdot u=u^2/2\).

    For \(1\lt u\le2\) the region missing is a triangle in the top-right corner with legs \(2-u\): area \(1-\tfrac12(2-u)^2\).

    This matches Example 6.3. Differentiating gives the triangular density \(f_U(u)=u\) on \([0,1]\) and \(2-u\) on \((1,2]\), drawn in Live figure B6.

  4. Write α for Test 2 and set it equal to α₁.

    \[ \alpha_2=P(U\gt c\mid\theta=0)=1-F_U(c)=0.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_U(c)=0.95 \]

  5. Decide which piece of the CDF to use. \(F_U(1)=1^2/2=0.50\lt0.95\), so \(c\) must lie in \((1,2]\).

  6. Solve.

    \[ 1-\frac{(2-c)^2}{2}=0.95 \]

    Subtract 1 from both sides and multiply by −1:

    \[ \frac{(2-c)^2}{2}=0.05 \]

    Multiply both sides by 2:

    \[ (2-c)^2=0.10 \]

    Take square roots. \(2-c=\pm\sqrt{0.10}\), and we keep the positive root because \(c\lt2\):

    \[ 2-c=\sqrt{0.10}=0.316228\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \class{dv}{c=2-\sqrt{0.10}\approx1.6838} \]

  7. Check by plugging back in.

    \[ \alpha_2=\frac{(2-1.683772)^2}{2}=\frac{(0.316228)^2}{2}=\frac{0.100000}{2}=0.05\ \checkmark \]
Answer

\(\class{dv}{c=2-\sqrt{0.10}\approx1.684}\). Both tests then have \(\alpha=0.05\).
